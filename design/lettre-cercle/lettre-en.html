Dear friend,

Please find attached the brochure and the presentation note of Democracy Together. The brochure is available in three languages for now, and it will grow with the project.

And the project is off to an excellent start!

It is only beginning, and yet it already draws a great deal of interest, often real enthusiasm. Almost everyone we have spoken to said yes straight away.

One question keeps coming back: “What can I do?”

Here are a few answers. They will change as the project grows: after the launch will come the time to consolidate and expand, and the ways to get involved will become more precise, more specialised or broader.

To begin with, we are creating the Circle of Friends of Democracy Together. We would be delighted if you would join it.

As a member of the Circle, you can:

  1. Tell us what concerns you

    Share, quite simply, the questions you care about most: those that deserve to be thought through together, and that might one day lead to action, or those on which you have something to contribute. A few lines are enough, a longer text is just as welcome, and you can come back as often as you like.

    Your texts stay in a private area of the website, open only to the active participants in the initiative, Friends like you among them. They will help us choose the topics of the coming think tanks. And if a discussion starts on yours, we will be glad to have you take part.

    Open the Circle’s private area: democracy-together.vercel.app/en/espace-membre/cercle

  2. Write for KOHOP

    If you feel like going further, you can publish a more developed text on KOHOP (the brochure explains the format). It will be published in open access, with the names and comments of two or three reviewers you choose yourself. The text remains yours: you can reuse it wherever you wish.

    It is often simpler than people think. If you have already thought or written about a subject, in an article or a book for instance, all it takes is to draw one strong idea from it and argue it clearly, so that it reaches a wider audience.

    Discover KOHOP: democracy-together.vercel.app/en/kohop

  3. Spread the word

    If you know trusted friends who might be interested in the initiative, tell them about it and pass on this letter, the brochure and the presentation note. Why not encourage them, too, to write for KOHOP?

  4. Hear from us

    A newsletter will regularly tell you what we are doing, where we stand and what we are preparing.

  5. And support us

    In every way possible (financially too, through personal contributions, introductions, recommendations, and so on).

    Make a donation: democracy-together.vercel.app/en/don

How to join

Simply fill in a short online request and attach a brief CV (under ten lines) as a PDF. It will be read by the president of the association (or the president’s team). As soon as it is accepted, you receive an e-mail to enter the Circle’s area.

Join the Circle: democracy-together.vercel.app/en/cercle

The State facing complexity: red alert

Author: Philippe Kourilsky

This text was published in French on 21 October 2026 on telos.eu. It is published on KOHOP with the agreement of Telos. Translated from the French.

We know the world is complex, but do we realise how much the nature of that complexity has changed? Since computers began beating the world’s best players at chess and go, we have had proof that the complexity of human artefacts exceeds the capacities of the human brain. The complexity of systems (often represented as networks) can be measured by the number and diversity of the elements that make them up and of the interactions that link them. By that measure, the complexity of the objects we make (cars, aircraft, computers, AI, etc.) has increased considerably (ref. 1).

The elephant in the room is that, in parallel, and largely because of the exponential growth of data (“big data”), social complexity has exploded. And this is only the beginning. Without suitable tools, we are now unable to manage increasingly complex social systems. Businesses are affected, but so are public decision-making and the State as a whole.

The inability to manage complexity is one of the reasons why so many countries and democracies are in difficulty.

The French case

This is particularly serious in a country as state-run and centralised as ours, where, for example, the proliferation of regulations only adds to the very complexity it seeks to control. The French State has lost control of public debt and is no longer able to carry out reforms that are both plainly necessary and indispensable (pensions, health, justice, etc.). It is increasingly seen as oppressive, paralysing, powerless, incomprehensible and lacking credibility. Worse, citizens are losing confidence in its democratic foundations. Their desperate quest for simplicity and efficiency makes them ever more receptive to the siren songs of populism, whose growing appeal makes the task of democratic government harder still.

A radical overhaul of the State is needed, without any doubt, and urgently. One major condition is to avoid the fatal mistake of believing that everything can be achieved through simplification. Complexity can be pruned (for example in our bloated and redundant body of legislation). But it cannot be abolished, whatever many populists say. Since reality is complex by nature, trying to erase that complexity amounts to denying reality itself.

We must do the opposite: make complexity intelligible and manageable. It can be done. Our cars, soon semi-autonomous, are more and more complex. Yet anyone can drive them. How? By adding layers of complexity (automation) that make them intelligible and manageable. They have not been simplified: they have been “simplexified”. And their users have kept, and even increased, their freedom and their ability to use them.

Mutatis mutandis, the same goes for the management of the State. Politics and administration can no longer rest only on the art of governing and political know-how. They demand more and more technical expertise, and that expertise changes very quickly. Are political and administrative leaders trained and selected to meet this challenge? No. How skills are defined, and how leaders are trained and selected, is a fundamental problem. It must be tackled head-on, regardless of political affiliation.

The problematic role of AI

Today, a growing share of the tools needed to manage complexity relies on AI. Yet these technologies are overwhelmingly designed and developed abroad. The result is ideological and commercial dependencies that are incompatible with national or European sovereignty, and that pose real risks to democracy. Beyond military applications, this is a matter of national defence. A distinction must be drawn here between commercial AI and AI designed to serve the State and democratic institutions, which must remain under the control of the State.

The specifications and the scale of the investment required are of a quite different order. Developing robust models (or “digital twins”) of ministries and administrations would make it possible to better anticipate their possible trajectories, and therefore to reform them, while optimising how they work and making them more intelligible to citizens. This requires neither the colossal computing power nor the most sophisticated developments pursued by foreign companies whose stock market valuations run into trillions of dollars.

The effect on our lives of the growing complexity of the world of objects and social groups has many other dimensions. As crucial elections approach in our country, the considerations above are essential and must be taken into account as soon as possible.

1. Philippe Kourilsky, La démocratie malade de la complexité [Democracy sick with complexity], Chemins de Tr@verse, 2026.

3. Jean-Pierre Roth

Comment on Philippe Kourilsky’s contribution: a few levers for reforming the French State.

Lucidity and urgency: these are the two features that strike me on reading Philippe Kourilsky’s contribution. The lucidity is the author’s; I feel no need to add to it here. The urgency is that of the situation. How can our country overcome its bureaucratic overdose, which, even when recognised, stiffens every attempt at a solution? Simplexification is one way. It still has to be put into practice.

Let us start with what would affect many citizens and can be done without major prior reform.

The tax administration is, in my view, well run, adequately resourced and certainly a pioneer in the use of AI. Following its example, we could begin to change mindsets. The first step would be to publish its results systematically and clearly. The fight against fraud would feature there, but on the same footing as the cases in which the administration has done justice to taxpayers or granted them relief. Next, the same administration could take on a few great taboos that weigh on our budget. The one I care about is the tax relief and other advantages granted to research. Many industrial companies probably do not need them, research being part of their very purpose. As for many others … Forty years ago I was myself co-owner of a small technology company bought from MATRA, in which the “statement of failure” form was filled in at the same time as the grant application. Repayment of the grant was thus always postponed. This annual lie was quite in keeping with a general abandonment of performance.

A major field in which simplexification can change behaviour is the law. The actors involved are well known, and they are plainly running out of breath. They are intellectually capable of using AI to help the nation return to what we lost years ago while still invoking it like a mantra: the rule of law. Let us open the Légifrance website and focus on the codes, the core of legislation. We are told there are 76 of them, offering 18,000 pages to read. Who can know and understand them, who can foresee how they interfere with one another? All this is well known to judges and court clerks, who cope by stretching the time taken for investigation and decision to absurd lengths. The judicialisation of families and businesses has thus led to denial of justice and to inertia. For intelligence is not enough. To change things, the people of the judiciary lack an internal leadership that would give them the courage to do so. Reforming 76 codes through parliamentary debate would take half a century, assuming it were pursued consistently. But what about making them ONE single code? Its contradictions and its tatters would fall away of their own accord. Well directed, AI can do it.

Intelligence, national security and defence have one great advantage over other administrations: their staff are recruited and managed methodically, and their leaders exercise genuine (and entirely legitimate) authority. They have one handicap: their results are invisible in peacetime. Yet they cost everyone dearly, and everyone ought to understand that their capability is a key to our international action and to our protection in the broad sense. It is time to build a unified picture of them that makes sense. That is not the case today. The nuclear deterrent and its resources remain the closed professional sector that De Gaulle built. At the other end, the citizen army no longer exists, for good reasons, but some are floating plans in which it is hard to tell whether they would be scout camps or civics classes. In between, the professional army works, but it is disconcerted, its budget unbalanced by the loss of our “Françafrique”, where it housed half of its training at lower cost. As for the “services”, they have become popular thanks to a television series. How do these disparate pieces protect us, how do they prepare France for serious military action, for example in the North or East of Europe? It is time this became understandable, if we want the French nation to support both the financial effort and the moral rearmament that are needed here.

In the long run we are all dead: we owe this useful reminder to Keynes. May I then, to finish, speak of the medium term? For it to be worthwhile, at least two of the actions above would need to have had a happy impact, and minds would need to have begun to believe in success. When that happens, if it happens, it is clearly the education system that will have to be tackled. It will be a heavy task. Its staff are overabundant and demoralised, its leaders have not a shred of authority. Nothing is possible there, in my view, unless something in the structure of this soft monster is broken. I will not elaborate here. I will simply note that the disappointment caused by the allocation system known as Parcoursup is not, in itself, the problem of higher education. It is a crossing point between A and B. Abolishing it would mend neither A nor B.

To conclude, allow me to return to urgency. In his concise and illuminating book La démocratie malade de la complexité (Éditions Chemins de traverse, Paris 2026), Philippe Kourilsky links it to the imminence of the presidential election due in May 2027. I agree with him that we would do well to launch one or more reforms before that deadline, in the hope of increasing the likelihood that they will be continued afterwards. But as to the overall diagnosis, I am struck by how closely the picture painted by Kourilsky matches the image that General De Gaulle gave of the France of 1940, in his letter° to the journalist Pertinax of 17 May 1943: “… The fact is that it had become almost impossible to govern and to command in France other than in appearance, amid the chronic paralysis of the State.”

We have the choice between a jolt and asphyxiation.

21 November 2026
Jean-Pierre Roth

° letter quoted by the eminent historian Julian Jackson in his book The Fall of France: The Nazi Invasion of 1940 (Oxford University Press, 2003). The quotation is translated here from the French.
